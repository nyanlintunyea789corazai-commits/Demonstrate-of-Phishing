Notice that you never typed anything except your username and password. Everything else your browser handed over automatically. A real attacker also sees your IP address, which reveals your approximate city and internet provider.

What happens next in a real attack

  1. Within minutes, the attacker (often a script) tries your username and password on the real bank's website.
  2. Credential stuffing: they try the same password on email, social media and shopping sites, because most people reuse passwords.
  3. Email takeover is the jackpot: with your email they can reset the password on almost every other account you own.
  4. Your details are sold in bulk lists, so other criminals can use them weeks or months later.
  5. The browser and time zone details help them look like you when logging in, so fewer alarms go off.

The red flags you could have spotted

  1. The web address was wrong. examp1ebank-secure-login.account-verify.co. The real site's name is the part just before the last dot-ending. Here that is account-verify.co, which has nothing to do with the bank. Words like "secure" or "login" in a URL prove nothing.
  2. A lookalike name. "Examp1eBank" uses the number 1 instead of the letter l. Attackers also swap rn for m, 0 for o, or use foreign letters that look identical.
  3. "Not secure" / no HTTPS. The address started with http:// and the browser warned you. Be careful though: many phishing sites do use HTTPS now. The padlock only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.
  4. Urgency and fear. "Unusual activity… sign in within 24 hours to avoid suspension." Pressure is designed to make you act before you think.
  5. You arrived from a link. Real banks rarely ask you to sign in from a link in an email or text. The safe habit is to type the address yourself or use the official app.
  6. The logo and colours looked right. That is not a good sign. Copying a brand's look takes an attacker a few minutes, so looks are never proof.

How to protect yourself

Use a password managerIt only fills your password on the exact real domain. If it doesn't offer to fill, stop and check the URL.
Turn on MFAA second step (app code, security key, passkey) means a stolen password alone isn't enough. Security keys and passkeys can't be phished at all.
Never reuse passwordsOne phished password should unlock one account, not all of them.
Go directType the address yourself or use a bookmark or the official app instead of clicking links in messages.
Read the real domainLook at the name just before .com, .co, etc. Everything to the left of it can be faked.
Slow down on urgencyThreats, deadlines and prizes are the classic tricks. Pause and verify through a channel you trust.

If you did fall for one

  1. Change that password immediately on the real site, and anywhere else you used it.
  2. Turn on MFA if it wasn't on already, and sign out of other sessions.
  3. Contact the bank or service using the number on your card or their official site.
  4. Report the phishing message (e.g. "Report phishing" in your email app).
  5. Watch your accounts for unusual activity over the next few weeks.
